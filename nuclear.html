Nuclear energy

#Nuclearphysicistsdonotmakejustnuclearbombs

Do nuclear physicists actually make bombs?

This is the most frequent question I hear when I say I study nuclear physics.

And the answer, at least for me and for most nuclear physicists, is simple: no, I do not make bombs — and I do not want to work on them in the future.

Nuclear physics is actually a very broad field. It refers to everything that has to do with the atomic nucleus — and, more deeply, with what happens inside it. And once you start looking inside the nucleus, you realize that there is far more going on than one might expect.

With protons and neutrons — particles we cannot even see — we are able to do an incredible range of things. Beyond the most well-known applications, such as nuclear energy and nuclear weapons, nuclear physics is used in:

  • medicine, for cancer treatments and medical imaging
  • industry and agriculture, through the use of isotopes
  • materials science, for example in ion implantation
  • archaeology and geology, with radiocarbon dating

So the real question becomes: how can something so small be so powerful?

The nucleus is the dense inner part of an atom, made of protons and neutrons. These particles cannot be seen with our own eyes and feel very far from our everyday experience. Because of this, it is natural to be skeptical — how can we trust something that we cannot even see?

And in some sense, that caution is justified. If handled incorrectly, nuclear materials can indeed be dangerous and harmful. But when understood and used properly, they become powerful tools — capable of producing energy, advancing science, and even treating diseases like cancer.

So what is nuclear energy?

Nuclear energy is the energy released from changes in the nucleus of an atom. This can happen through processes such as nuclear fission, where a heavy nucleus splits into smaller ones, or nuclear fusion, where light nuclei combine to form a heavier one.
